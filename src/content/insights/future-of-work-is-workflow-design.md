The future of work is often presented as a forecast about which jobs will disappear. The more useful executive question is how work will be recomposed: which tasks become automated, which decisions require deeper expertise, which new roles connect technology to outcomes, and how people learn while the system is changing.

The OECD’s 2026 work on AI and skills puts the emphasis where leaders can act: skills only create value when people can use them inside a changed workflow, with the authority, practice, and support to make good decisions. That is not a curriculum problem alone. It is a design problem involving roles, workflow, authority, tools, incentives, and time to practice.

## Start with the role, not the course

A skills taxonomy is useful, but a person does not apply an abstract skill. They apply it in a moment: reviewing evidence, resolving an exception, coaching a colleague, configuring a system, or making a tradeoff.

For each role that will change, map the work before and after the technology arrives. What will the person stop doing? What will they need to notice earlier? Which decisions move closer to the front line? What evidence will they need? What new failure modes become possible?

This produces a more honest capability plan. It might call for training, but it might also call for a clearer decision right, a better interface, protected practice time, a new manager routine, or a service-level measure that reinforces the behavior.

## Learning has to be inside the operating rhythm

Organizations learn faster when the work itself creates feedback. A team can review the exceptions from an AI-assisted process, share the judgment that resolved them, update the guidance, and improve the workflow in the next release. That is more powerful than sending everyone to a course and hoping the old system creates space for the new capability.

For education and workforce leaders, this suggests a shift from completion rates toward demonstrated capability: can the person perform the new task safely, explain the decision, and help the organization improve the system around it?

## Skills-first is an operating choice

The OECD’s conclusion shifts the leadership question from “Which jobs disappear?” to “Which tasks change, and how will people participate in the redesign?” Skills-first hiring, reskilling, and talent progression are useful only when the operating model gives people authority, practice, and a fair way to have their experience recognized.

## What leaders can do now

Choose one role that will change materially in the next year. Write the future-state workflow, the decisions, the evidence, the practice environment, and the manager behaviors before buying a training package. Then run a short pilot with a real measure of confidence, quality, and time-to-proficiency.
