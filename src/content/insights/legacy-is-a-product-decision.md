In 2025, the U.S. Government Accountability Office identified 69 federal legacy systems in need of modernization. The headline can make modernization sound like an inventory problem. It is more consequential than that: every legacy system is also a service, a dependency, a funding stream, a set of working habits, and a promise made to the people who rely on it.

That is why a modernization program can spend years producing architecture diagrams without reducing risk. The diagrams may be accurate, but the decisions are not yet operational.

## Stop treating the current state as one thing

“Legacy” is not a sufficient disposition. A system can be old and strategically differentiating. It can be modern and operationally fragile. It can be expensive because it is poorly governed, or expensive because the mission truly requires a high-assurance capability.

We use four questions to make the portfolio legible:

1. What outcome does this system make possible, and who is accountable for it?
2. Which capabilities are unique, and which are accidental artifacts of the current implementation?
3. What evidence would tell us that a replacement is safer than continued operation?
4. What change can users and operators absorb without breaking the service?

Those questions produce a more useful map than a technology age field. They also connect funding decisions to service continuity and workforce readiness.

## Modernization needs an operating rhythm

GAO’s recommendations emphasize documented plans, milestones, and disposition decisions. We would add a management layer: a modernization office that can make tradeoffs visible every month, keep architecture tied to mission outcomes, and surface decisions that need executive attention before they become schedule surprises.

The strongest programs create a sequence of releases. They stabilize the service, expose interfaces, retire one dependency, move one decision to a new platform, and measure whether the people responsible for the outcome can actually operate the new system. This is ITIL change management at enterprise scale: less ceremony for its own sake, more control over the moments when a system and an organization change together.

## The Global Enterprise view

Our work has included solution strategy for federal agencies, enterprise architecture, and long-running change management across complex service environments. We bring the mission, the service model, the technical decision, and the adoption path into the same room.

The goal is not to modernize everything. It is to make the next irreversible decision with enough clarity that the organization can keep moving.
